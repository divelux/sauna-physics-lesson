Answer key: Why is the top bench hotter?

For the teacher

Accept any reasoned prediction. Score the explanations, not the predictions.

1. The puzzle

First guesses are not scored. Keep them: students revise them in the exit ticket.

2. Part A: colored water

Warm red water: rose from the bottom toward the surface and spread out across the top, then faded as it mixed.

Ice-cold blue water: sank and spread out along the bottom.

Why: warm water is less dense than the room-temperature water around it, so it rises. Cold water is denser, so it sinks. If the warm water did not rise, it cooled in the dropper; that is a fair observation to discuss.

3. Part B: the air in our classroom

Answers depend on the class data. Full credit for a table and a plot that match the readings and an honest sentence about the pattern, including "no clear difference" if that is what the data show. The high reading may be a little warmer than the floor reading; in an evenly heated room the difference can be too small to measure.

Things that make a small difference hard to measure (any two): the room is heated evenly; air moves through vents, doors and windows; sunlight or a heater near one thermometer; the thermometers' own error; one reading each instead of several.

4. Model it

Warm box: particles farther apart, longer arrows. Cold box: particles closer together, shorter arrows. Same number of particles in each, and the particles are the same size in both. A drawing with bigger particles in the warm box shows the misconception "particles expand"; address it.

5. Explain the sauna

  1. 105 cm (hottest), then 60 cm, then 30 cm (gentlest).
  2. Air warmed by the heater rises to the ceiling, moves across it, cools a little, sinks near the far wall and flows back along the floor to the heater: a convection loop.
  3. Exit ticket, full credit: the heater gives energy to the air near it, so its particles move faster and spread farther apart. That air is less dense, so it rises, and cooler, denser air sinks to take its place. This convection keeps the warmest air near the ceiling, so the top bench sits in hotter air than the bottom one. Partial credit: "hot air rises" with no particles or density.

Standards

Builds toward MS-PS1-4 (particle model of temperature) and MS-ESS2-6 (unequal heating drives circulation). Uses PS3.A (temperature and average kinetic energy) and PS3.B (energy moves from hotter to colder regions). See the lesson plan for the full text.
