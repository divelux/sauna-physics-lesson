# Why Is the Top Bench Hotter? A Lesson on Warm Air and Convection

**Grades 6–8 · one 50-minute class · physical science · free, CC BY 4.0**

Students explain a real-world puzzle: in a sauna, the top bench is the hottest and the bottom bench is gentler. They see warm water rise and cold water sink in a safe colored-water model, measure the air at three heights in their own classroom, and draw a particle model of why warm fluids rise. No open flame, no boiling water, no one needs to visit a sauna.

Files: `handout.pdf` (student worksheet, 2 pages), `answer-key.pdf` (1 page), `assets/sauna-convection.svg` (diagram for the board). Web version: <https://divelux.github.io/sauna-physics-lesson/>

---

## Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

1. Describe what happens to particle motion and spacing when a liquid or a gas is warmed or cooled.
2. Use that model to explain why warmer water or air rises and cooler water or air sinks.
3. Describe a convection loop in a room, and use it to explain why higher benches in a sauna are hotter.
4. Collect and compare temperature readings at different heights, and say what could make a small difference hard to measure.

## The phenomenon

Show the diagram `assets/sauna-convection.svg` or a photo of a sauna with stepped benches. Read this line from HUUM, a sauna heater maker:

> "Heat rises upward: the top bench is the hottest, while the lower bench feels gentler."

HUUM gives typical bench heights in today's saunas as 30 cm, 60 cm and 105 cm above the floor (about 12, 24 and 41 inches). Ask: *It is the same room, with the same air. Why would the air by the top bench be hotter than the air by the bottom bench?*

Source: HUUM, [Planning a sauna bench: practical tips and ideas](https://huum.eu/planning-a-sauna-bench-practical-tips-and-ideas/). Saunafin, a Finnish sauna company, says the same: "The upper sauna benches will always be hotter than the lower ones as the heated air rises" ([How to optimize heat circulation in large saunas](https://www.saunafin.com/blog/how-to-optimize-heat-circulation-in-large-saunas/)).

## Materials

**For each group of 3–4 students (Part A)**

- A clear container that holds at least 2 liters (a plastic storage box, a large clear jar or a cut-down 2-liter bottle)
- Room-temperature tap water to fill it, set out 30 minutes before class so it is still and at room temperature
- 2 small cups, one with **warm tap water** and red food coloring, one with **ice water** and blue food coloring
- 2 plastic droppers or transfer pipettes
- Paper towels, a tray to catch spills

**For the class (Part B)**

- 3 identical digital thermometers (or one, moved and given 3 minutes at each spot)
- Masking tape, a meter stick or a yardstick
- A shelf or cabinet top about 2 m (6 ft) high where the **teacher** can place a thermometer without climbing on furniture

**Copies:** one `handout.pdf` per student.

## Safety

- **No heat sources.** No kettles, hot plates, candles, lighters, heat lamps or light bulbs. The warmest thing in the lesson is warm water from the tap.
- **Warm, not hot.** The teacher fills the warm cups and checks that the water is comfortable to hold a finger in. If it is too hot to keep a finger in, it is too hot for the lesson; add cold water.
- **Spills.** Water on the floor is a slip hazard; wipe it up at once. Food coloring stains clothes and desks; use trays.
- **No climbing.** Only the teacher places the high thermometer, from the floor, with a meter stick or tape if needed.
- **Thermometers.** Use digital or alcohol (red) thermometers, never mercury.

## Lesson sequence (50 minutes)

### 1. Engage: the sauna puzzle (5 min)

Show the diagram and the HUUM quote. Students write a first guess on the handout: *Why is the top bench hotter?* Collect a few ideas without judging them. Ask students to keep their guess; they will revise it at the end.

### 2. Explore A: colored water (15 min)

1. Before class, set up Part B (below) so the thermometers have time to settle.
2. Each group fills its clear container with the still, room-temperature water and waits a minute for it to stop moving.
3. **Predict** on the handout: what will the warm red water do? The cold blue water?
4. **Warm red:** fill a dropper with warm red water, lower the tip gently to the bottom of the container, and squeeze very slowly. Watch for 30 seconds without touching the container. Draw what happens.
5. **Cold blue:** fill a dropper with ice-cold blue water, hold the tip just under the surface, and squeeze slowly. Watch and draw.
6. Optional: do both at once at opposite ends of the container and watch for a loop.

*What students usually see:* the warm red water rises toward the surface and spreads out; the cold blue water sinks and spreads along the bottom. If the warm water does not rise, it has cooled in the dropper; refill and work faster.

### 3. Explore B: our classroom's air (10 min, readings taken during Part A)

1. At the start of class, place the three thermometers: one on the floor, one at desk height (about 75 cm / 30 in), one up high (about 2 m / 6 ft, placed by the teacher). Keep them out of sunlight and away from heaters, vents and windows.
2. After at least 10 minutes, a student reads each one. Record all three on the board.
3. Students copy the readings into the table on the handout and plot temperature against height.

*What to expect:* the high reading may be a little warmer than the floor reading. In an evenly heated room the difference can be too small to measure. That is a useful result too. Talk about why: a classroom is heated much more evenly than a sauna, air moves through vents and doors, and a thermometer has its own small error. Read the thermometer's accuracy from its box if you have it.

### 4. Explain: a particle model (10 min)

Guide the class to these ideas, then have students draw the model on the handout.

- Water and air are made of particles that are always moving.
- **Warming** makes the particles move faster on average (that is what a higher temperature means). Faster particles push each other a little farther apart, so the same amount of matter takes up a bit more space: it becomes **less dense**.
- **Cooling** slows the particles. They stay closer together, so the fluid is **denser**.
- In a fluid, the less dense part rises and the denser part sinks, the way a cork rises in water. This movement of a warm fluid carrying energy is called **convection**.
- Energy flows from hotter places to colder ones, so the warm water that rose slowly cools and mixes, and the colors fade.

Back to the sauna: the heater warms the air next to it. That air becomes less dense and rises to the ceiling, spreads across, cools a little along the walls, sinks and flows back along the floor toward the heater: a **convection loop** (show the diagram). The air settles in layers, warmest at the top. The top bench sits in warmer air than the bottom bench, so it feels hotter.

### 5. Elaborate: bench heights (5 min)

Using HUUM's typical bench heights (30, 60 and 105 cm), students rank the three benches from hottest to gentlest and explain their ranking with the particle model. Extension question: why might someone new to the sauna start on the lowest bench? (HUUM notes that people often start on the lower bench and move up.)

### 6. Evaluate: exit ticket (5 min)

Students revise their first guess from step 1 into a short explanation that uses the words **particles**, **density** and **convection**.

## Differentiation

- **Support:** give a word bank (particles, faster, slower, spread out, closer together, density, rises, sinks, convection). Let students describe the model with arrows and labels rather than full sentences.
- **Stretch:** ask how a sauna builder could use the convection loop (where to put the heater, the benches, the vents). Or plan a fair test for Part B: how could we be sure a one-degree difference is real?
- **Language learners:** the diagram and the colored-water drawings carry most of the meaning; pair students to describe them aloud before writing.

## Common misconceptions

| Students may say | A better way to say it |
| --- | --- |
| "Heat rises." | *Warm air* (or warm water) rises because it is less dense. Heat is energy; it moves from hot to cold in any direction. |
| "Cold sinks because cold is heavy." | Cold water or air is *denser*: the same space holds more matter. |
| "The particles get bigger when they warm up." | The particles stay the same size. They move faster and spread a little farther apart. |
| "The top of the room is closer to the Sun." | The difference comes from convection inside the room, not from the Sun. |

## Answer key

The same key, laid out for printing, is in `answer-key.pdf`. Numbers follow the sections of the handout.

**1. The puzzle.** First guesses are not scored. Students revise them in the exit ticket.

**2. Part A: colored water.** Warm red water rose from the bottom toward the surface and spread out across the top, then faded as it mixed. Ice-cold blue water sank and spread out along the bottom. Why: warm water is less dense than the room-temperature water around it, so it rises; cold water is denser, so it sinks. If the warm water did not rise, it cooled in the dropper; that is a fair observation to discuss.

**3. Part B: the air in our classroom.** Answers depend on the class data. Full credit for a table and a plot that match the readings and an honest sentence about the pattern, including "no clear difference" if that is what the data show. Things that make a small difference hard to measure (any two): the room is heated evenly; air moves through vents, doors and windows; sunlight or a heater near one thermometer; the thermometers' own error; one reading each instead of several.

**4. Model it.** Warm box: particles farther apart, longer arrows. Cold box: particles closer together, shorter arrows. The same number of particles in each, and the particles are the same size in both. Bigger particles in the warm box show the misconception "particles expand"; address it.

**5. Explain the sauna.**

1. 105 cm (hottest), then 60 cm, then 30 cm (gentlest).
2. Air warmed by the heater rises to the ceiling, moves across it, cools a little, sinks near the far wall and flows back along the floor to the heater: a convection loop.
3. Exit ticket, full credit: the heater gives energy to the air near it, so its particles move faster and spread farther apart. That air is less dense, so it rises, and cooler, denser air sinks to take its place. This convection keeps the warmest air near the ceiling, so the top bench sits in hotter air than the bottom one. Partial credit: "hot air rises" with no particles or density.
